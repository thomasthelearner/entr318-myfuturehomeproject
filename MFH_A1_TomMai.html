Me and My Financial Vision & Mission

Tom Mai

Most days you'll find me somewhere between a lecture hall and a spreadsheet, working through the same dream list I've been building since I first sat down to think seriously about money. As of this October 2036, I'm finishing my final stretch at BYU–Hawaii, right here in Lāʻie — and I still have a habit of pointing excitedly at buildings I one day hope to either live in, work in, or own a piece of.

The most meaningful financial lessons I’ve learned didn't come from my childhood background, but from observing the environment around me and learning from the people I encountered. My true financial maturity was forged after I got married. Being blessed with a spouse who is deeply passionate about personal finance completely shifted my perspective, pushing me to learn, adapt, and handle money with much greater purpose and intention.

Looking back at major life milestones—from moving to Hawaiʻi to navigate a new environment, to getting married and starting a family—I can clearly see how these experiences shaped my core character. My strengths in spirituality, kindness, generosity, and leadership naturally guide how I view money: as a tool to bring joy to others and take care of my family. However, this same generosity can sometimes double as a weakness, occasionally leading to a lack of financial discipline or overspending. Recognizing these dynamics between my strengths and blind spots is the first step toward becoming a wiser, more balanced steward of my resources.

Financial vision

I see my family financially secure enough to serve as senior, full-time missionaries together — free of debt, free of financial worry, and fully available to give our time to the Lord when that season of life arrives.

Financial mission

To save and invest consistently - 10% of our monthly income, year after year, until we've built a retirement fund strong enough to make that vision possible.

This project is my answer to "where am I headed?" — a single page I can return to, edit, and build on as my circumstances change. It combines a written narrative of the next ten years, a look at my future family, and an honest accounting of future income, and it's designed to grow alongside the budget and home-ownership plans I'll be adding in future assignments.

Section 2

My Path to the Future — October 2026 to October 2036

It's October 1, 2036. Looking back, the last ten years went by faster than I expected — and somehow, slower too, in all the best ways.

Oct 2026 – Apr 2028

I finished my Bachelor of Science in Computer Science at BYU–Hawaii, in Lāʻie. I chose that program because I wanted a foundation broad enough to pursue studies in Artificial Intelligence and machine learning — a decision that shaped everything that came after.

Apr – Oct 2028

Before starting work in the U.S., Linh and I returned to Vietnam for six months to sort out visas, reconnect with family, and travel.

2028 – 2030

I moved to the U.S. to work for an international technology company, while we kept The Mama's Loaf running back in Hawaiʻi. Those two years were about discipline: growing the business, investing part of every paycheck in the stock market, and setting aside savings earmarked specifically for a PhD.

2030

By 2030 we'd saved enough to make the leap. I enrolled in a PhD program in Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning curriculum at Brigham Young University in Provo, Utah, and Linh began BYU–Pathway Worldwide courses to sharpen the business and management skills she'd use running our company.

2030 – Dec 15, 2033

Those three years in Provo were some of the richest of my life. I worked as a research assistant to one of my professors, building AI and machine learning models and turning them into real applications. I earned my PhD on December 15, 2033. Spiritually, that season included ordinance work at the Salt Lake Temple, which became one of my favorite ways to spend a quiet evening.

Apr 5, 2034

I was hired as an Associate Professor at BYU–Hawaii, teaching in the AI and Machine Learning program — and we moved back home to Lāʻie, close enough to walk to the Laie Hawaii Temple again.

2034 – 2036

The last three years have been about teaching, and about why I wanted to teach in the first place. A doctor can save a life; a good teacher can shape one. I wanted to be that for someone, the way my own teachers were for me. I've served as a Sunday School teacher, and Linh and I are both quietly preparing — spiritually and financially — for a future season of full-time missionary service together.

Section 3

My Future Family

Family, more than anything else, is what this whole plan has been for. Linh and I married in Vietnam in 2023, and ours is a single-earner household by design: my income from BYU–Hawaii is the financial base, while Linh's business adds meaningful upside without requiring her to be away from our children all day.

We manage money jointly: one shared account for the household budget, a separate account for The Mama's Loaf, and a standing rule that any purchase over $200 gets discussed before it happens. Every month we sit down together, look at what came in and what went out, and adjust as needed.

Our traditions are simple and steady — family scripture study most mornings, a standing Sunday family home evening with Vietnamese students, and at least one trip back to Vietnam every couple of years to see parents, aunts, uncles, and cousins we don't get to see nearly often enough. Between my teaching schedule and Linh's business, weekends stay protected: church, family, and rest, in that order.

Section 4

My Future Work (Income)

Our family's employment vision is simple: be financially ready to serve the Lord, whenever and wherever He needs us. The mission tied to that vision is concrete — save $100,000 as a family, with the hope that it prepares us for a future calling as a Mission President couple, ideally in Vietnam.

My primary income comes from teaching three to four courses a semester, Monday through Friday, on a schedule set each term by my department chair. Because I work within a Church-sponsored university, the pace respects family and Church commitments in a way I don't take for granted — I'm rarely asked to sacrifice one for the other.

Linh's bakery and small-goods business brings in income that comes with a satisfaction beyond the dollar amount: the pride of watching something she built from scratch actually work.

I'd call myself genuinely satisfied with where we've landed. I work hard, but not at the expense of my family or my faith, and Linh's business gives her both purpose and flexibility. If anything, the biggest adjustment has been learning to feel settled with "enough" instead of always chasing more — which, looking back, might be the real financial lesson of these ten years.

Our family car

Section 10 · Housing

My Future House

Our vision is a safe home that supports our family, faith and teaching. Our mission is to live within our means in Hawaiʻi while building a rental investment in Vietnam.

Our home in Hawaiʻi

We assume we move into a three-bedroom BYU–Hawaii employee unit in April 2034. We live in Lāʻie, Hawaiʻi 96762, United States, close to work, school and the temple. The campus reference address is 55-220 Kulanui Street; our residential unit is not yet specified.

Our estimated rent is $2,048/month. We rent this home, so there is no U.S. mortgage. We budget $25/month for renters insurance, within the $500 insurance category: $30,000 contents, $300,000 liability, $12,000 loss-of-use and a $1,000 deductible. We would compare providers such as GEICO. The university insures the building; our own coverage protects our belongings and liability.

We choose a smaller apartment instead of the original villa idea. Our target is a $250,000 two-bedroom apartment in the An Phú area of Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, in the area formerly called District 2. We assume we have saved a separate $80,000 during 2028–2035: $75,000 for the down payment and $5,000 for buying costs. That means saving about $833/month over eight years. This money is spent at closing and is not still counted in our bank balance.
